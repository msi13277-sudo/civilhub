Why driveway grade is a compliance question, not just a number

A driveway that is too steep — or that changes grade too abruptly — will scrape the underside of a car, be unsafe in the wet, and fail to gain approval. In Australia, domestic driveway gradients are governed by AS/NZS 2890.1 (parking facilities / off-street car parking), which sets both a maximum grade and limits on how quickly the grade is allowed to change at crests and dips. This checker screens a driveway grade — and, optionally, a change of grade at a break point — against the limits commonly cited from that standard, so you can see early whether a design is likely to pass or need a transition. It answers the everyday question, "is this driveway too steep, and do I need a transition where it flattens out?"

Two things matter: maximum grade AND change of grade

Most people think only about the maximum grade (how steep the steepest section is). But the second factor — change of grade at a break point — is what actually causes vehicles to bottom out. Where a steep ramp meets a flatter section (at the top of a driveway, or in a dip at the gutter), the algebraic difference between the two grades matters. A ramp going from +12% up to −5% down has a change of grade of 17%, even though neither grade on its own is extreme. If that change happens over too short a distance, the car's front or rear overhang, or its belly, grounds out. That is why the standard limits change of grade separately, and why a transition (a short intermediate grade) is required where the change is too great.

  • A summit / crest (a hump — grade decreasing) risks grounding the belly of the vehicle.
  • A sag / dip (a valley — grade increasing) risks scraping the front and rear overhangs.

Commonly cited AS/NZS 2890.1 domestic limits

The key figures widely quoted for AS/NZS 2890.1:2004 are summarised in the table near the top of this page: a maximum domestic driveway grade of 1 in 4 (25%), a maximum change of grade without a transition of 12.5% at a crest and 15% at a sag, and a minimum transition length of about 2.0 m (often at half the grade change). The standard itself, and the ground-clearance vehicle templates in its appendices, take precedence for any real design — obtain the current standard rather than relying on the summary above.

The council overlay: often stricter than the standard

This is the part designers miss. Even where a driveway passes AS/NZS 2890.1, the local council or authority requirements usually take precedence and are frequently stricter. Many QLD councils prefer a working maximum closer to 1 in 5 = 20% and have their own standard drawings for driveway crossovers and vehicular access. A "PASS" against the cited standard is a screening result, not an approval — always check the applicable council standard drawing and planning scheme codes before finalising a crossover or access design.

Worked examples

  1. Steep suburban block. A driveway is proposed at 22%. That is within the 25% domestic maximum, so it screens as PASS against AS/NZS 2890.1 — but if the council caps grades at 20%, it fails the local requirement. This is exactly why the checker's PASS says "verify against council".
  2. Grade change at the garage. A ramp climbs at +14% then meets a level garage floor (0%). The change of grade is 14% at a summit, which exceeds the ~12.5% crest limit — a transition is required, so the tool returns REVIEW.
  3. Dip at the gutter. A driveway drops to a low point at the kerb and rises into the property: −10% to +8% is an 18% change at a sag, above the ~15% sag limit — again a transition is needed to avoid grounding the front overhang.

Common mistakes

  • Checking only the maximum grade. A driveway can be under 25% everywhere yet still bottom out at a sharp break. Always check change of grade at every break point.
  • Ignoring the sign of the grades. Change of grade is the algebraic difference. +10% to −5% is 15%, not 5%.
  • Assuming the standard is the whole story. Council conditions, sight distance at the frontage, and the crossover standard drawing all apply on top of AS/NZS 2890.1.
  • Forgetting the vehicle templates. The standard's ground-clearance checks (design vehicle templates) are the real test for grounding; a grade-only screen does not replace them.
